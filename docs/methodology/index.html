The investment case

The ranking starts from a written view of what a good mid-market investment in UK digital infrastructure looks like: a private business with £10–250m of sales and £2–15m of EBITDA, recurring revenue, healthy and steady margins, a balance sheet that can carry some debt, and an owner who could sell. Every filter and every point in the score comes from that case. Changing the case changes the ranking, which is why the method is published.

The score

Five factors, each worth a set number of points, add up to 100. How reliable the figures are is rated separately, so a company is not ranked higher just because its accounts are easier to read.

Repeat revenue up to 25 points

How much of the sales are likely to recur. Read from the accounts for the leading companies; estimated from the type of business for the rest.

Fits the case up to 20 points

How closely the business matches the investment case. Only companies confirmed to trade in the sector are ranked.

Right size up to 15 points

EBITDA (profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) in the £2–15m range most mid-market buyers target.

Profit margin up to 25 points

How high the EBITDA margin is and, where three or more years of accounts have been read, how steady it has been.

Balance sheet up to 15 points

Net assets, cash and margin headroom, reduced where interest cover is thin or net debt is above 1x EBITDA. A first check, not a full measure of how much a buyer could borrow.

Confidence in the figures

High figures tagged in the filing and three or more years read. Medium one weakness, such as fewer years read or an estimated input. Low figures read from the text of the accounts or by OCR, estimated inputs, or fewer than three years. Each company page lists the exact reasons. Confidence is shown beside the score and never added to it.

73 ranked companies have had their figures checked against the filing by hand, and 26 filings were corrected where the automatic reading was wrong.

Types of deal

Which kind of fund each company suits. Applied in order, the first match wins. The type sits beside the score and never changes it.

Steady compounder
Recurring-type revenue, margin of 10% or more, sales not falling and a steady margin. Suits: Core and core-plus infrastructure funds.
High-growth
Sales growing 20% a year or more over several years (25% if only one year is known). Suits: Growth and mid-market PE.
Fixable dip
Falling sales or a thin margin, with a named problem and a fix. Suits: Value-add infrastructure and special-situations funds.
Under pressure
Falling sales or a thin margin, and no fix identified yet. Suits: None until a fix is identified.
Growth infrastructure
Loss-making while building a network, but growing. Not ranked; on the watchlist. Suits: Growth infrastructure funds (loss-making while building).
Between types
Good on some tests, not others, for the reason given. Suits: Depends on the reason given.
Numbers to check
Something in the figures needs checking before the company can be typed. Suits: Fix the numbers first.

Deal readiness

Four checks for the top ten, shown as passes, partly or fails. They say whether a well-scored company is also a deal a mid-market buyer could do now.

Size
Likely deal size within £10–250m: passes inside the band, partly if it could be above or below, fails if probably outside it.
Buyable
An owner able and likely to sell: fails if bought recently, partly if only possible as a carve-out from a larger parent.
Price
Passes if a buyer could make 20% a year at the middle of the price range, partly if only at the low end, fails if not at all.
Figures
Passes with high confidence, partly with medium, fails with low.

Deal size and price

Likely deal size is EBITDA times a multiple range: 6.0–8.0x for repeat-revenue services, 5.0–7.0x for contractors, and a higher range for network owners such as towers and fibre, which infrastructure funds price well above service firms. Where a public deal value exists it is used instead. The price check adds net debt, cash conversion and a buyer's 20% return test for the top ten.

Sector placement

Each company sits in one sector by its main activity. Ranked companies are placed from a checked description of what they do; the rest by their industry code only, and marked so. Contractors that build networks for others form their own sector, because they are valued differently from network owners.

Weekly updates

Every Monday the ranked companies are checked for new Companies House filings: accounts, charges (security for loans), changes of directors and owners. New accounts are read and the ranking rebuilt. Charges and ownership changes are flagged on the market intelligence page as possible exit or refinancing signals. Current method: v3.1.

Limits

The ranking points to where to look and gives a first view on price. It is not a valuation and not advice. Open each limit for detail.

Price check is a first pass

Ranges come from listed peers and deal averages, not from deals in each company's niche. Debt and cash are read from each top-ten company's latest accounts, which can be up to two years old, and debt held by owners' holding companies is not visible.

Deal size is an estimate

EBITDA times a multiple range, or a public deal value where one exists. Network owners are priced on a higher range because infrastructure funds pay more for them.

Repeat revenue is partly read, partly estimated

For the leading companies it is read from the accounts (sales split, income paid in advance, contracts described), which rarely give renewal rates. For the rest it is judged from the type of business.

Balance sheet, not debt capacity

Net assets, cash, margin and interest cover where known. True debt capacity needs net debt, capital spending and cash conversion, which are read only for the top ten.

Some scanned accounts not read

Figures for 605 companies that file scanned accounts were read by text recognition (OCR) and are labelled "OCR, check". 237 of the 1,209 companies (20%) still have scanned accounts that could not be read, so good companies may be missing.

Sector check pending for some companies

13 profitable companies are waiting for a check that they really trade in the sector. An industry code alone is not enough to be ranked.

Demand splits are estimates

For the top ten, each company's sales are split across what they depend on, estimated from the accounts and public contracts; filings rarely give the split directly. Further down the list, a company with two named sources is assumed 70/30.

Accounts are historic

Companies House accounts are filed up to nine months after the year end, so the latest figures can be more than a year old. The ranking is rebuilt weekly, but the numbers change only when new accounts are filed.

Not a view on any company

Rankings measure fit with one written investment case for a mid-market buyer. A low rank says nothing about a company's quality, management or prospects.
